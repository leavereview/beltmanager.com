Two questions come up at every school sooner or later: how often should we test, and what should we charge for it? Test too often and belts stop meaning much. Test too rarely and students drift away between ranks. Charge too little and the test costs you money; charge without explaining it and parents start to call it a belt factory.

This guide gives you the published rules from the bodies that publish them, a way to pick a testing cycle that fits your schedule, and a cost-based way to set a fee. Every figure taken from a governing body is linked in the sources at the end, with the date we checked it. If you want the software side, our [dojo management software](/dojo-management-software/) page shows how BeltManager builds a ready-to-test list from your attendance records.

## What Sets Your Testing Calendar

Five things decide how often you can sensibly test. Work through them in this order.

1. **Your association's rules.** If you certify ranks through a national or international body, its minimums come first. Some publish exact periods. Others leave color belts entirely to the school.
2. **How often students train.** A minimum of 24 classes means three months for a student who trains twice a week and six months for one who trains once a week.
3. **Age.** Shorter steps help keep younger children engaged. That is why some systems put stripes or degrees between full belts for juniors.
4. **Belt level.** The gap between tests normally grows as students move up. A white belt and a brown belt should not be on the same clock.
5. **Instructor time.** A test takes an afternoon to run and more to prepare. Pick a cycle you can run well every time.

| | 3rd to 4th dan | 3 years between certificates | [4] |

A few notes on reading the table:

- **IBJJF shortens some adult periods** for students who held kids' or juvenile belts and for world champions. The periods above are the standard ones. The IBJJF also says the adoption of its degree (stripe) system below black belt "is at the professor's discretion".
- **For kids' stripes, the IBJJF suggests three systems**, not one: quarterly (three degrees per belt), every four months (two degrees per belt) or monthly (11 degrees per belt). Each is a suggestion a professor can adopt or not.
- **USA Judo calls time in grade "a minimum benchmark"** and adds that "promotion is not automatic even with time-in-grade". Dan applications go through the state organization's promotion committee.
- **USA Karate holds dan testing "2-3 times a year"** at its signature events.
- **Kukkiwon awards a poom, not a dan, to younger students.** The guide we used lists poom ranks for ages up to and including 15.
- **These documents cover black belt ranks** for judo, karate and taekwondo. For color belts in those arts, your school or your organization sets the pace.

If you teach an art with no belts, as many kickboxing and Muay Thai gyms do, none of this binds you. Some gyms add their own levels; our [kickboxing and Muay Thai gym software](/kickboxing-gym-software/) page covers how that works.

## How to Pick a Testing Cycle

There are three common ways to run it.

| Cycle | How it works | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed calendar | Tests on set dates, such as every three months. Students who meet the minimums are invited. | Kids' classes, larger schools, planning the year | Students who just miss a date wait a full cycle |
| When ready | The instructor promotes a student when they meet the standard, often in class. | BJJ, small adult classes | Harder to explain to parents; easy to overlook quiet students |
| Stripes plus belts | Small steps (stripes or tips) in class, with a formal test for the full belt | Juniors, long gaps between belts | More to track for every student |

To turn that into dates:

1. **Write down the classes and time you require at each belt.** These are your numbers unless your association sets them.
2. **Divide the classes by how often students train.** *Example: you ask for 24 classes at white belt and beginners train twice a week. 24 ÷ 2 = 12 weeks, so a test every three months fits. If you tested every two months, almost nobody would be eligible.*
3. **Lengthen the gap as rank rises.** If lower belts test every cycle, higher belts might be eligible every second or third cycle.
4. **Publish the dates for the whole year.** Families plan around them, and you avoid a test in the week everyone is away.
5. **Check eligibility a month ahead,** not the night before. That leaves time to talk to students who are short.

The checking is where an accurate attendance record earns its keep. If you are still on paper, our free [attendance sheet template](/attendance-sheet-template/) has a belt column and a monthly total for exactly this job.

## What to Charge for a Belt Test

### What the federations publish

These are fees charged by the organization itself, as published. They are reference points, not a survey of what schools charge.

| Body | What the fee covers | Published fee | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ISKF Alaska (Shotokan karate) | Kyu test | $45 for the first family member, $25 for each additional family member, $5 late fee for payment on the day | [5] |
| USA Judo | Dan promotion | Shodan $150, nidan $200, sandan $250 | [2] |
| USA Karate | Dan certification | 1st, 2nd or 3rd dan $250; 4th or 5th dan $500; junior dan (under 18) $150 | [3] |

Two things stand out. The black belt fees here are several times the kyu test fee; a national body is certifying the rank. And one organization builds in a lower fee for additional family members, which is worth copying if you teach a lot of siblings.

### A cost-based way to set your own fee

We are not going to quote a "typical" fee for color belt tests, because we have no sourced figure for one. A fee you can explain is better than one you copied. Build it from your costs:

1. **List the direct costs per student:** the new belt, the certificate, boards or other materials.
2. **List the shared costs for the day:** instructor and examiner hours, room rental if you rent extra space, and any fee your association charges you.
3. **Divide the shared costs by the number of students you expect to test.**
4. **Add the two together.** That is your break-even fee.
5. **Decide on a margin,** if any, and round to a sensible number.

*Worked example. These figures are made up to show the arithmetic; put in your own.*

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Belt, per student | $8 |
| Certificate, per student | $2 |
| Boards, per student | $2 |
| **Direct cost per student** | **$12** |
| Two instructors for three hours at $25 an hour | $150 |
| Shared cost per student, with 20 students testing | $7.50 |
| **Break-even fee per student** | **$19.50** |

In this example, a $20 fee covers the test and nothing more. With 10 students testing, the shared cost doubles to $15 each and break-even becomes $27, which is why a small school may need a higher fee or a longer cycle.

### Fee per test, or included in tuition?

- **Separate fee.** Tuition stays lower and only students who test pay. You need to explain what it covers.
- **Included in tuition.** Simpler for families, and no one feels they are buying a belt. Your tuition has to carry the cost.
- **Cost only.** Charge for the belt and certificate and absorb the rest.

Whichever you choose, put it in writing when a student enrolls, and never let the fee decide who passes. A student who is not ready should not test, paid or not.

## Students Under the Attendance Minimum

Every cycle, a few students will be close but short. Handle it the same way each time.

- **Tell them early.** A month before the test, not when the list goes up.
- **Offer a route.** Make-up classes before the date, or a place on the next test.
- **Keep the minimum a minimum.** Meeting it makes a student eligible to be assessed. It does not mean they pass. USA Judo's wording is a good one to borrow: promotion is "not automatic even with time-in-grade".
- **Write exceptions down.** If an instructor waives the minimum for a student who trained elsewhere or missed time through injury, note who approved it and why.
- **Don't test a student to fail them.** If they are not ready, hold them back before the day.

## A Simple Scoring Sheet

A one-page sheet keeps examiners consistent and gives you something to show a parent afterward. Change the rows to fit your art.

| Area | What the examiner looks for | Score (1 to 3) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basics | Stances, strikes, blocks, falls or movement for this belt | | |
| Forms | Kata, poomsae or patterns required at this belt | | |
| Partner work | Drills, one-steps, throws or positional work | | |
| Sparring or rolling | Control, timing and safety | | |
| Knowledge | Terminology, etiquette, history | | |
| Attitude | Effort, focus, respect for partners | | |

Score each area 1 (not yet), 2 (meets the standard) or 3 (above the standard). Decide your pass rule before the test, for example no area scored 1. Have two examiners score separately and compare. File the sheets with the attendance record.
