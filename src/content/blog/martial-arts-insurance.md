Before the first student steps on your mat, three things should be in place: insurance, a waiver and a business structure. Each protects you in a different way, and none of them replaces the other two. This guide explains what each one does, what the martial arts insurance programs actually publish about cover and price, how waiver law changes from state to state, and what it costs to form an LLC.

Every legal point, price and limit below is linked in the sources at the end, with the date we checked it. This is general information, not legal or insurance advice. Talk to a licensed insurance agent and an attorney in your state before you open. BeltManager has no relationship with any insurer named here, and naming a program is not a recommendation. If you are also choosing [martial arts software](/martial-arts-software/), that page covers what BeltManager does for students, belts and attendance.

## Three Layers, and What Each One Does Not Do

| Layer | What it does | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance | Pays for a covered claim and the cost of defending it, up to the policy limit | Cover things the policy excludes, or amounts above the limit |
| Waiver | Shows the student or parent was told about the risks and, where state law allows, releases some claims | Work the same way in every state, or release everything |
| LLC or corporation | Keeps business debts and claims apart from your personal assets "in most instances" [16] | Pay a claim or a legal bill |

A school with a waiver and no insurance has nothing to pay a lawyer with. A school with insurance and no waiver has skipped a layer that its state's law may give weight to. A sole proprietor with both still has personal assets on the line. Plan for all three.

## What Martial Arts Insurance Covers

The Small Business Administration (SBA) describes the standard types of business insurance [1]:

- **General liability** protects against financial loss from claims such as bodily injury and property damage.
- **Professional liability** "protects against financial loss as a result of malpractice, errors, and negligence."
- **Commercial property** protects "against loss and damage to company property due to a wide variety of events."
- **A business owner's policy** is "an insurance package that combines all of the typical coverage options into one bundle."

Programs sold to martial arts schools and instructors package these with cover for injury to participants. This is what four program pages listed when we checked them:

| Program | Who it is for | Coverages the page lists | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| K&K Insurance, martial arts schools | Schools | General liability including damage to premises rented to you; hired and non-owned auto liability; professional liability; bodily injury to participants; medical payments for participants. Optional: equipment and contents, hosted tournament, sexual abuse or molestation liability, excess liability | [2] |
| Markel, martial arts | Martial arts businesses | General liability, accident medical, professional liability, property. Optional: abuse and molestation, competitions and exhibitions | [4] |
| Sadler Sports & Recreation, martial arts and self-defense instructors | Individual instructors aged 18 or over who work on an independent contract basis | General liability, bodily injury to participants, professional liability | [3] |
| Thimble, martial arts instructor | Individual instructors | Third-party bodily injury, third-party property damage, defense costs | [6] |

Two things stand out. First, cover for an instructor and cover for a school are different products. Sadler's instructor program excludes "Operation, Ownership, or Management of any Facility" [3], so it does not fit an owner with a lease. Second, sexual abuse or molestation liability is listed as an option on the school programs [2] [4] and as an exclusion on the Sadler instructor program [3]. If you teach children, ask about it by name.

## What Martial Arts Insurance Costs, and the Limits on Offer

Most pages do not publish a price. K&K and Markel publish no prices or limits for schools and quote after underwriting [2] [4]. Here is everything the pages we opened do publish:

| Program | Published limits | Published price | Valid | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sadler, instructors (traditional martial arts) | Each occurrence $1,000,000 to $5,000,000 in five options; general aggregate $5,000,000 on every option | $375 a year for the $1,000,000 option, rising to $1,303 a year for $5,000,000, per instructor, plus a $20 fee per application | January 1 to December 31, 2026 | [3] |
| Next Insurance, martial arts instructor | General liability per occurrence: $500,000, $1 million or $2 million, depending on the plan | The page gives a figure without saying whether it is monthly or annual, so we have not repeated it | Checked November 21, 2026 | [5] |
| Thimble, martial arts instructor | Policy limit of $1,000,000 or $2,000,000 | None published | Checked November 21, 2026 | [6] |
| K&K, schools | None published | None published | Brochure valid March 1, 2026 to February 28, 2027 | [2] |

We have not given a "typical" premium for a school, because none of these pages publishes one and we will not invent one. What a school pays depends on things the insurer asks about. K&K's page, for example, sends schools over 3,000 square feet, and schools that offer activities other than martial arts, to a fuller application [2]. Get at least two quotes for the same limits so you can compare like with like.

Your lease may set limits you have to carry. Read the insurance clause before you ask for quotes.

## Exclusions to Ask About

Policies differ most in what they leave out. These exclusions and conditions appear on the pages we checked:

- **MMA and Muay Thai events.** K&K lists both as eligible styles with a condition: "Coverage for these styles is available only to instruction and training programs." Events, competitions and tournaments of those styles are excluded [2].
- **Tournaments.** K&K lists a hosted tournament as an optional extra [2]. Sadler's instructor program does not cover tournaments or competitions [3].
- **Boxing and wrestling.** K&K lists contact or sparring boxing and the sport of wrestling as ineligible [2]. Sadler also lists contact or sparring boxing as ineligible [3].
- **Weapons.** Sadler's instructor program excludes "Use of Sharpened/Bladed Weapons" [3].
- **Your own staff.** Thimble says "injuries to your staff aren't covered under our policy" [6]. Staff injuries are what workers' compensation is for.
- **Your equipment.** Thimble says damage to equipment such as weapons and sparring gear is not covered under its liability policy [6]. That needs property cover.

Every one of these pages says the policy itself has the full list. Ask for the exclusions in writing and read them against what you actually teach: styles, weapons, sparring rules, birthday parties, off-site demonstrations and anything you host.

## Workers' Compensation Depends on Your State

Once you have employees, workers' compensation becomes a question, and each state answers it differently. The SBA puts it briefly: "Laws requiring insurance vary by state" [1]. Three examples:

| State | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | In Texas, "private employers can choose to carry workers' compensation insurance coverage, but it is not required in most cases." Employers that go without must report that to the state | [7] |
| Florida | Outside construction, the law applies to private employment "in which four or more employees are employed by the same employer" | [8] |
| California | "If a business employs one or more employees, it must satisfy the requirement of the law." | [9] |

Whether your instructors count as employees is its own question. Our guide to [instructor pay, hiring and payroll costs](/blog/martial-arts-instructor-pay/) covers the employee or contractor tests.

## Occurrence or Claims-Made?

This is the one piece of policy wording worth learning before you compare quotes. With an occurrence policy, "the CGL policy in effect at the time the alleged injury or property damage occurred is the policy that covers that event" [10]. With a claims-made policy, the insurer on risk when the claim is made is the one that responds [10].

It matters for a martial arts school because an injury claim can arrive long after the class where it happened. Ask which type you are being quoted, and what happens to past incidents if you change insurer.

## Questions to Ask an Agent

1. Is this a policy for a school or for an individual instructor?
2. Which of my styles and activities are covered, and which are excluded?
3. Are participant injuries covered, and is there a medical payments limit?
4. Is sexual abuse or molestation liability included, optional or excluded?
5. Are tournaments, demonstrations and events away from the school covered?
6. Is the policy occurrence or claims-made?
7. What are the each-occurrence and aggregate limits, and do they meet my lease?
8. Is damage to the premises I rent covered?
9. Is my equipment covered?
10. Does the policy say anything about waivers?

On the last point: none of the insurer pages we opened makes signed waivers a published condition of cover. A policy or application may still ask, so put the question.

## Waivers: The Law Changes at the State Line

A liability waiver is a contract, and the rules for it are set state by state. The same form can be enforceable in one state, limited in the next and void for a child in a third. These five examples come from the statutes and decisions themselves:

| State | What the source says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | An agreement "purporting to release liability for future gross negligence, generally is unenforceable as a matter of public policy" (state Supreme Court, 2007) | [11] |
| Montana | A written waiver signed before a sport or recreational activity may cover ordinary negligence and inherent risks, but it must state the known inherent risks and carry a set statement in bold that begins "By signing this document you may be waiving your legal right to a jury trial" | [12] |
| Florida | A parent may waive a child's claims against a commercial activity provider for injury from an "inherent risk" of the activity. The waiver must include a notice in capital letters, at least 5 points larger than the other text, including "YOU ARE AGREEING TO LET YOUR MINOR CHILD ENGAGE IN A POTENTIALLY DANGEROUS ACTIVITY." | [13] |
| Colorado | "A parent of a child may, on behalf of the child, release or waive the child's prospective claim for negligence." The statute does not allow a parent to waive claims for willful and wanton, reckless or grossly negligent acts | [14] |
| New Jersey | The court held that "a parent's execution of a pre-injury release of a minor's future tort claims arising out of the use of a commercial recreational facility is unenforceable" (state Supreme Court, 2006). The same decision upheld the parent's agreement to arbitrate | [15] |

Five states are not a survey, and we have not listed any state whose statute or decision we could not open and read. But four practical points follow from these examples:

1. **Do not use a generic template unchanged.** Montana and Florida both require specific words, and a form without them misses what the statute asks for [12] [13].
2. **Minors are a separate question.** Colorado and New Jersey reach opposite results on a parent's signature [14] [15]. If you teach children, this is the part to have reviewed.
3. **A waiver does not reach everything.** California and Colorado both stop at gross negligence [11] [14]. Florida's parental waiver covers inherent risks, and the statute's definition of a participant whose conduct counts as an inherent risk "does not include the activity provider or its owners, affiliates, employees, or agents" [13].
4. **Keep the signed copy.** A waiver you cannot produce is not much use. Record who signed, for which student, and when.

Have an attorney in your state write or review the waiver. It is a one-time cost for a document every student signs.

## LLC Basics

If you start teaching without forming a company, you are a sole proprietor. The SBA is direct about what that means: "You can be held personally liable for the debts and obligations of the business" [16]. A limited liability company changes that: "LLCs protect you from personal liability in most instances" [16]. The SBA also says the protection has limits and that business insurance can fill the gaps [16], which is why an LLC goes alongside insurance and does not replace it.

**Tax.** Forming an LLC does not by itself change how you are taxed. The IRS treats an LLC with one member as "disregarded as separate from its owner" for federal income tax, and an LLC with two or more members as a partnership, unless the LLC files Form 8832 to choose otherwise [17]. Ask a CPA which treatment suits you.

**Cost.** You form an LLC by filing with your state, and fees vary widely. Four examples:

| State | Fee to form an LLC | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | $70 to file Articles of Organization, plus an annual LLC tax of $800 | [18] [19] |
| Texas | $300 to file a certificate of formation | [20] |
| Florida | $100 to file articles of organization, plus $25 to designate a registered agent | [21] |
| Kentucky | $40 to file Articles of Organization | [22] |

These are state filing fees only. Annual reports, local business licenses and anything you pay a formation service or attorney are extra.

**Three more things to know:**

- **An EIN is free.** The IRS says "You never have to pay a fee for an EIN" [23]. Be wary of websites that charge for one.
- **Beneficial ownership reports are no longer required of US companies.** FinCEN states that US companies are exempt and "are no longer required to file BOI reports", under a final rule effective August 14, 2026 [24]. Older guides, and some government pages that have not been updated, still describe a filing deadline.
- **Licenses are mostly state and local.** Federal licenses apply to activities a federal agency regulates. States and local governments set their own licenses and permits [25]. Check with your city or county before you sign a lease.

## A Start-Up Checklist

1. Choose a business structure and file with your state.
2. Get an EIN from the IRS, free.
3. Check state and local license and permit requirements for your address.
4. Read the insurance clause in your lease.
5. Get at least two insurance quotes for the same limits, and read the exclusions.
6. If you will have employees, find out your state's workers' compensation rule.
7. Have an attorney in your state write or review your waiver, including the version for minors.
8. Decide how you will collect and keep signed waivers.
9. Keep attendance records from the first class.
